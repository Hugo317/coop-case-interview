Coop_Case_Presentation.pptx — Speaker Notes
================================================

27 slides total. The case brief gives you a 30-minute slot — that's roughly a minute per slide if
you hit all of them, which is tight. Section dividers (slides 4, 10, 16, 22) take 10-15 seconds
each ("just say the title and move on"). Spend your real time on the finding slides. If you're
short on time, the safest slides to skim or cut live are: Q1's basket/timing slide (6), Q2's
MSC/fair-trade slide (13), and Q3's category-affinity slide (20) — all solid but not load-bearing
for the overall story.

Every SEK figure in the deck is a MONTHLY run-rate (2-month sample ÷ 2), not a 2-month total —
say "per month" out loud whenever you cite a number, it's on-brand with how the deck labels them.


1. TITLE SLIDE
--------------
Just introduce yourself and the scope: 2 months of real (anonymized) Coop receipt data, 2 stores,
April-May 2020. One line: "I looked at four things — how customers shop across channels, how
sustainable our assortment is, what actually makes a customer valuable, and what our discount
spend is really doing for us."


2. AGENDA
---------
Read the four section titles, one sentence each. Don't over-explain here — the sections speak for
themselves once you're in them. This slide exists so the room knows the shape of the next 25
minutes.


3. DATA & METHODOLOGY
----------------------
Say what "profit" means throughout (revenue minus cost, excl. VAT — not real net income, no
overhead/wages/rent factored in). Flag the monthly-run-rate convention once here so you don't have
to repeat it every slide. Then hit the caveat box: the accompanying description file claims roughly
double our actual row/receipt counts — the file itself is complete and un-corrupted, so treat this
as a documentation mismatch on their end, not missing data on ours. Says "we caught this and it
doesn't change our numbers" — a credibility beat, not a confession.


4. SECTION DIVIDER — 01 CHANNEL BEHAVIOR
------------------------------------------
Just read the question. "How do offline, online, and omni-channel customers shop, and what sets
them apart."


5. ONLINE EXISTS AT STORE A ONLY — NEVER ON WEEKENDS
-------------------------------------------------------
Lead with the surprise: online purchasing is exactly zero, every single weekend, across the whole
2-month window — not a dip, zero. Verified directly against all 18 weekend dates in the data. Frame
it as operational, not demand-driven — nothing in the data suggests customers don't want weekend
online, just that Coop isn't offering it. This sets up the opportunity slide later.


6. ONLINE SHOPPERS BUY MORE, AND BUY AHEAD OF MEALS
--------------------------------------------------------
Quick one: bigger baskets online, more items per trip — consistent with planned/bulk shopping
rather than a quick top-up. Timing clusters around 9am and 3pm, before meals — people are
planning ahead, not impulse-buying online. Keep this slide brief, it's supporting color for the
bigger story.


7. OMNI-CHANNEL HOUSEHOLDS ARE ~2.6X MORE PROFITABLE
---------------------------------------------------------
This is a headline slide — slow down here. Omni-channel households are worth 2.56x as much profit
per month as everyone else, and that gap barely moves (2.59x) after controlling for buying power —
so it's a real behavioral signal, not just "omni customers are richer." But flag the catch:
only ~0.55% of households are omni today, and part of that low number is structural — Store B
customers can never qualify since online doesn't exist there at all. That's the bridge into the
next slide.


8. SIZING THE OPPORTUNITY
----------------------------
Two scenarios, both clearly naive/directional — say that out loud before showing the numbers, so
nobody in the room treats them as a forecast. Left chart: if weekends matched weekday online
performance at Store A, roughly +415K SEK/month. Right chart: if omni-channel adoption reached 75%
company-wide, using today's per-household profit gap, roughly +4.7M SEK/month uplift. Immediately
follow with the caveat: today's 113 omni households are small and self-selected, this ignores
fulfillment cost and cannibalization — treat this as "size of the prize," not a number to put in a
budget.


9. Q1 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
----------------------------
Four asks: test weekend online at Store A (economics already work on weekdays), investigate
extending online to Store B (closes a structural gap), build a retention play specifically for
existing omni households (protect what's already proven valuable), and re-baseline the omni rate
internally against Store-A-eligible households only (the raw company-wide number understates real
adoption).


10. SECTION DIVIDER — 02 SUSTAINABILITY
-------------------------------------------
Read the question and move on.


11. BASELINE ADOPTION IS LOW — AND ONE FLAG UNDER-COUNTS IT
------------------------------------------------------------------
State the baseline numbers quickly (krav 6.1%, organic 3.8%, etc.), then explicitly exclude
no_lactose from "sustainability" — it's a dietary label, not environmental, and say why that
distinction matters. Then the real finding, framed correctly: only 34.5% of KRAV items are also
flagged organic in the data — but KRAV certification requires meeting organic standards as a floor,
so a KRAV item IS organic in substance either way. The takeaway isn't "KRAV and organic are
inconsistent," it's "the organic flag alone under-counts true coverage — use organic-or-KRAV
instead." Frame this as a data-completeness fix, not a labeling contradiction — and flag that it
changes a downstream finding (next few slides).


12. SUSTAINABILITY LIVES ALMOST ENTIRELY IN FRESH PRODUCE
----------------------------------------------------------------
Fruit, vegetables, and vegetarian lead; packaged/pantry goods are near-zero. On meat (4.1% on the
organic flag alone), don't call it a confirmed gap on this slide — say it's worth re-checking once
KRAV coverage is included, since that's exactly the correction the previous slide just made.


13. MSC AND FAIR TRADE ONLY MAKE SENSE IN THEIR OWN CATEGORIES
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Quick methodology note: these two flags only apply to narrow slices of the catalog, so we measured
adoption within the relevant categories, not against everything. MSC is at 66.6% within fish — right
where you'd expect. Fair trade tops out around 2.4% even in its best categories. Keep this slide
short.


14. THE BIGGEST OPPORTUNITY IS SUPPLY, NOT DEMAND
-------------------------------------------------------
This is the actionable one, and it changed once we corrected the organic-flag issue from slide 11.
Lead with that: cheese and frozen fish LOOKED like zero-organic gaps under the old lens, but both
already have real KRAV coverage, so they're not actually gaps. The real remaining ones: in-store
bread (~129K SEK/month), functional drinks (~73K), salad sides (~54K), and deli-counter meats
(~51K) — all genuinely at zero organic-or-KRAV coverage. Land the point: customers are already
buying heavily in these categories, so this is Coop's own assortment gap to close, not a customer
behavior problem.


15. Q2 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Close the assortment gap in the four REAL categories (bread, functional drinks, salad sides, deli
meats) — explicitly not cheese or frozen fish. Fix the organic flag's under-tagging internally,
since it understates Coop's real organic assortment everywhere, not just in these four categories.
Re-check meat once KRAV is included before calling it a gap. And promote (don't fix) MSC in fish
since adoption is already strong there.


16. SECTION DIVIDER — 03 CUSTOMER SEGMENTATION
----------------------------------------------------
Read the question and move on.


17. DEMOGRAPHIC SEGMENTS BARELY PREDICT VALUE
----------------------------------------------------
Set up the surprise: every MOSAIC lifestyle segment's profit share tracks its household share
almost exactly — avg profit per household per month ranges only 179-217 SEK across all 14 segments.
Say it plainly: MOSAIC group does not tell you who your valuable customers are.


18. BUYING-POWER CLASS DOESN'T TRACK SPEND — AND MUCH OF IT IS UNCLASSIFIED
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The best "wait, what?" moment in the deck: low-buying-power households average MORE profit per
month (209 SEK) than very-high-buying-power households (201 SEK). Backwards from what you'd expect.
Then the caveat: 17.4% of households — worth ~18% of profit — have no buying-power classification
at all, over 30x the equivalent gap in MOSAIC. Nearly 1 in 5 households isn't represented if this
field gets used for targeting.


19. WHAT ACTUALLY PREDICTS VALUE: PLAIN BEHAVIORAL CONCENTRATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------
The payoff slide for this section. Forget demographics — rank households by actual spend and the
top 10% drive 47.9% of profit, the top 50% drive 94.1%. Half of Coop's customers contribute almost
nothing. This is the number worth a dedicated loyalty/retention program, not another demographic
cut.


20. MOSAIC DOES SHOW WHAT SEGMENTS BUY — JUST NOT HOW MUCH
------------------------------------------------------------------
Give MOSAIC its due here so the room doesn't think you're saying "throw it away." It's a real
signal for category preference — rural traditionalists over-index hard on tobacco, family-centered
followers on flowers, high-spend villa households on beauty/health. Useful for assortment and
local marketing, just not for finding your best customers.


21. Q3 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Stop leading with MOSAIC/buying-power for value targeting, build a simple behavioral (RFM-style)
segment instead, keep MOSAIC for assortment/marketing use, and go investigate the top 10% of
households directly since demographics don't explain why they're valuable.


22. SECTION DIVIDER — 04 DISCOUNT ECONOMICS
------------------------------------------------
Read the question. Frame this section as "a couple of extra things we found interesting while
digging into the open-ended part of the brief" — it's bonus material, not a core ask, but worth the
room's time.


23. DISCOUNTS COST LITTLE TO OFFER — A CONVERTED CUSTOMER IS WORTH REAL MONEY
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two ideas on this slide, land them in order. First: the marginal cost of OFFERING a discount is
close to zero — no fixed spend like an ad campaign, Coop only pays out when someone actually
redeems it. Second: what's a converted customer actually worth — a household that goes from
single-channel to omni is worth +310 SEK/month more in profit, ongoing. Then the honest caveat,
don't skip it: our own analysis found that the simple correlation between discount usage and
becoming omni mostly disappears once you control for how often someone shops. So this is the
prize IF a conversion nudge works, not proof it will — which is exactly why a cheap pilot makes
sense here: low cost to test, real upside if it works.


24. DISCOUNT SPEND ALREADY SKEWS TOWARD LOWER-VALUE CUSTOMERS
------------------------------------------------------------------
Second half of the discount story. Top 10% of households drive 47.9% of profit but get only 35.0%
of discount spend — discounts are LESS concentrated than value, not more. Right chart: discount
intensity drops cleanly from 24.3% of revenue for the lowest-value quartile down to 7.2% for the
highest. Land the "so what": Coop isn't wasting discount budget on customers who'd buy anyway — the
existing pattern already looks like it's being used as an acquisition/retention tool on marginal
customers, which is the efficient way to run it.


25. Q4 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Run a controlled pilot on the cross-channel discount idea (near-zero cost, ~310 SEK/month upside if
it converts someone), don't rebalance the discount budget toward top-tier customers since today's
allocation already looks efficient, start tracking discount ROI by value tier instead of just in
aggregate, and reuse the same mechanism for the Q4.3 finding — nudging low-margin-anchored loyal
customers toward higher-margin substitutes.


26. SYNTHESIS
----------------
One closing line per section, in order — this slide now maps 1:1 to the four sections, so just
walk down it: (01) omni-channel households are worth ~2.6x more, and the biggest blocker is
self-inflicted (no weekend online, no Store-B online); (02) the sustainability gap is supply, not
demand — bread, functional drinks, salad sides, deli meats have no organic-or-KRAV options, and the
organic flag itself needs fixing since it under-counts; (03) behavior beats demographics — MOSAIC
and buying-power don't predict value, plain spend concentration does (top 10% = ~48% of profit);
(04) discount spend is already efficient (skews toward marginal customers), and because offering
one costs almost nothing, it's a cheap lever worth testing further.


27. TACK! / CLOSING
-----------------------
Thank the room, open for questions. Nothing more needed here — if you want a strong closing line,
something like: "The clearest theme across all of this: we don't need better demographic data, we
need to trust the behavioral data we already have."
