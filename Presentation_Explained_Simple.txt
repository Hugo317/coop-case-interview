Coop_Case_Presentation.pptx — Explained in Plain Language
================================================================

This walks through every slide and explains, in simple terms, what it's actually showing and why
it matters. No jargon. If a number says "per month," remember: we only had 2 months of real data,
so we took the 2-month total and divided by 2 to get an average month. It's an estimate, not a
guarantee every month looks the same.


1. TITLE SLIDE
--------------
Just the cover page. Says what this is: insights from real Coop receipt data, 2 stores, 2 months
(April-May 2020).


2. AGENDA
---------
A table of contents. Four topics: how people shop (online vs in-store), how eco-friendly our
products are, what actually makes a customer valuable, and what our discounts are really doing.


3. DATA & METHODOLOGY
----------------------
Explains the ground rules. "Profit" here just means: money customers paid, minus what the product
cost Coop — it does NOT include rent, salaries, electricity, etc., so it's bigger than Coop's real
profit. Also flags that the description file that came with the data claims about twice as many
rows as we actually have — we checked, our file isn't broken, the description document is just
wrong or outdated.


4. SECTION DIVIDER — CHANNEL BEHAVIOR
------------------------------------------
Just a title card. No content, just says "now we're talking about how people shop."


5. ONLINE EXISTS AT STORE A ONLY — NEVER ON WEEKENDS
-------------------------------------------------------
The big finding: Coop's online shop only works at ONE of the two stores, and even there, NOBODY
can buy online on a Saturday or Sunday — not "fewer people," literally zero, every single weekend
in the data. This isn't customers choosing not to — the option simply isn't there on weekends.


6. ONLINE SHOPPERS BUY MORE, AND BUY AHEAD OF MEALS
--------------------------------------------------------
People who shop online buy bigger carts (more stuff, more money) than people who shop in-store.
Also, online orders happen mostly around 9am and 3pm — before breakfast/dinner — meaning people
are planning ahead, not just grabbing something on a whim.


7. OMNI-CHANNEL HOUSEHOLDS ARE ~2.6X MORE PROFITABLE
---------------------------------------------------------
"Omni-channel" = a household that shops BOTH online and in-store (not just one or the other).
These customers are worth 2.6 times more money to Coop per month than everyone else. We double-
checked this isn't just because they happen to be richer households — the gap holds up either way.
The catch: almost nobody (0.55%) is currently an omni-channel customer, partly because Store B
doesn't even offer online, so those customers can never become omni-channel no matter what they do.


8. SIZING THE OPPORTUNITY
----------------------------
Two "what if" estimates, clearly labeled as rough guesses, not promises:
- What if weekends worked online like weekdays do? Maybe +415,000 SEK extra profit per month.
- What if 3 out of 4 customers became omni-channel (both online+in-store)? Maybe +4.7 million SEK
  extra profit per month.
Both numbers assume things stay simple and ignore real-world costs (like the cost of running
weekend delivery) — think of them as "how big could this be," not a business plan.


9. Q1 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
----------------------------
What to actually do about all this: try turning on weekend online sales at Store A, look into
bringing online shopping to Store B too, take extra good care of the customers who already shop
both ways (they're valuable, don't lose them), and when talking about "how many customers are
omni-channel," only count people who actually had the option — otherwise the number looks
artificially low.


10. SECTION DIVIDER — SUSTAINABILITY
-------------------------------------------
Title card. Now we're talking about eco-friendly/organic products.


11. BASELINE ADOPTION IS LOW — AND ONE FLAG UNDER-COUNTS IT
------------------------------------------------------------------
Only a small slice of what Coop sells is labeled organic, eco, or similar (roughly 4-6% of
purchases). We also looked into something that seemed like a contradiction: only about a third of
products with the "KRAV" organic certification are ALSO separately labeled "organic" in the data.
Turns out this isn't really a mistake — KRAV certification actually REQUIRES a product to already
meet organic standards (KRAV is stricter, built on top of organic rules). So a KRAV product IS
organic, whether or not someone also ticked a separate "organic" box for it in the system. The real
lesson: Coop's "organic" label alone undercounts how much of the assortment is actually
organic-equivalent — count KRAV products as organic too, and fix the tagging so this doesn't keep
happening.


12. SUSTAINABILITY LIVES ALMOST ENTIRELY IN FRESH PRODUCE
----------------------------------------------------------------
Fruits, vegetables, and vegetarian foods have the most organic options. Packaged/processed foods
have almost none. Meat looked low too (4.1%), but given what we just found about KRAV, that needs
a second look before calling it a real gap.


13. MSC AND FAIR TRADE ONLY MAKE SENSE IN THEIR OWN CATEGORIES
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Quick technical note: "MSC" (sustainable fishing label) only applies to fish, and "Fair Trade"
mostly applies to things like coffee and chocolate. Within fish specifically, sustainable
labeling is actually pretty common (66%) — that part's working well already.


14. THE BIGGEST OPPORTUNITY IS SUPPLY, NOT DEMAND
-------------------------------------------------------
This slide got corrected once we accounted for the KRAV finding from slide 11. Cheese and frozen
fish LOOKED like they had zero organic options — but once you count KRAV products as organic too,
they actually already have some. The real gaps (still genuinely zero, even counting KRAV) are:
in-store bakery bread, functional/energy drinks, salad sides, and deli-counter meats — all
popular, all with no organic-equivalent option at all. This isn't customers refusing to buy
organic — Coop simply isn't offering the choice in these specific categories yet.


15. Q2 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Add organic options to the 4 REAL gap categories (bread, functional drinks, salad sides, deli
meats) — not cheese or frozen fish, which already have organic-equivalent options via KRAV. Fix
the "organic" label so it also counts KRAV products, since right now it's undercounting Coop's
real organic assortment everywhere, not just in these 4 spots. Double check meat once KRAV is
counted in. And since sustainable fish is already doing well, just advertise it more rather than
trying to "fix" it.


16. SECTION DIVIDER — CUSTOMER SEGMENTATION
----------------------------------------------------
Title card. Now: what actually makes some customers more valuable than others?


17. DEMOGRAPHIC SEGMENTS BARELY PREDICT VALUE
----------------------------------------------------
Coop has "customer type" labels bought from an outside company (like "Metropolitan Pioneers" or
"Rural Traditionalists") based on where people live and their lifestyle. Turns out these labels
barely tell you anything about how much money a customer actually brings in — all the groups
spend roughly the same amount per month. These labels are not a good way to find your best
customers.


18. BUYING-POWER CLASS DOESN'T TRACK SPEND — AND MUCH OF IT IS UNCLASSIFIED
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Even weirder: households labeled "low income" actually spend slightly MORE at Coop per month than
households labeled "very high income." The label meant to predict wealth doesn't even predict
spending in the right direction. On top of that, nearly 1 in 5 households don't have this label at
all (marked "unknown") — so it's missing a lot of people too.


19. WHAT ACTUALLY PREDICTS VALUE: PLAIN BEHAVIORAL CONCENTRATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the real answer to "who are our best customers": just look at who actually spends the
most. The top 10% of customers (by actual spending) bring in almost half (47.9%) of all profit.
The top half of customers bring in 94% of profit — meaning the bottom half barely matters
financially. Forget fancy labels — just track who's actually spending.


20. MOSAIC DOES SHOW WHAT SEGMENTS BUY — JUST NOT HOW MUCH
------------------------------------------------------------------
To be fair to those "customer type" labels: they ARE useful for something — predicting WHAT
people buy, even if not HOW MUCH they spend. E.g., rural households buy a lot more tobacco than
average, family-oriented households buy a lot more flowers. Good for deciding what to stock or
advertise locally, just not good for finding your top spenders.


21. Q3 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Stop using those outside "customer type" labels to guess who your best customers are — use actual
spending data instead. Build a simple system that just tracks who spends the most and treat them
well. Still use the customer-type labels for deciding what products to stock where. And go study
that top 10% of spenders directly to understand what makes them special.


22. SECTION DIVIDER — DISCOUNT ECONOMICS
------------------------------------------------
Title card. This is bonus material (not one of the three main questions) — extra things we found
interesting about how discounts work.


23. DISCOUNTS COST LITTLE TO OFFER — A CONVERTED CUSTOMER IS WORTH REAL MONEY
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two ideas here. First: offering a discount barely costs Coop anything upfront — unlike running an
ad campaign (which costs money whether it works or not), a discount only "costs" something when a
customer actually uses it. Second: if you could turn a single-channel customer (only online OR
only in-store) into an omni-channel customer (both), that's worth about +310 SEK extra profit per
month, every month, going forward. BUT — important honesty check — we also found that just giving
people more discounts doesn't reliably make them switch channels on its own, based on the data we
have. So this is "here's the prize if it works," not "discounts definitely cause this." Worth
actually testing with a small experiment since it's so cheap to try.


24. DISCOUNT SPEND ALREADY SKEWS TOWARD LOWER-VALUE CUSTOMERS
------------------------------------------------------------------
Good news buried in the numbers: Coop isn't wasting its discount budget on customers who'd shop
there anyway. The biggest spenders (top 10%) get a smaller slice of discounts (35%) than their
slice of profit (47.9%) — meaning discounts are already going more toward the customers who need
a nudge, not the loyal ones who don't. That's actually efficient.


25. Q4 RECOMMENDED ACTIONS
------------------------------
Try a small test: offer a special discount to single-channel customers specifically to get them to
try the other channel, and compare results against customers who didn't get the offer. Don't shift
discount money toward big spenders — the current setup already looks smart. Start tracking how well
discounts work for different customer groups instead of just overall. And use this same cheap-discount
idea to nudge loyal customers who buy low-profit items toward higher-profit alternatives.


26. SYNTHESIS (SUMMARY OF EVERYTHING)
------------------------------------------
One big takeaway per topic:
- Shopping channels: customers who use both online and in-store are much more valuable, but Coop
  itself is blocking growth here (no weekend online, no online at Store B).
- Sustainability: the problem is Coop not offering organic options in certain categories, not
  customers refusing to buy them.
- Customer types: fancy outside "customer profile" labels don't predict who's valuable — actual
  spending does.
- Discounts: they're already being used efficiently, and because they're so cheap to offer, they're
  worth testing further as a tool (like nudging people to try online shopping).
